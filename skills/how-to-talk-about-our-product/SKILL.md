# Feedback Library Messaging Guide

This skill defines how the company talks about Feedback Library, for every team, every format, every audience. It is the single source of truth for messaging.

The examples use Example Feedback, a fictional customer feedback platform, and its research product, Feedback Library. Competitor names are fictional too. Replace the product, the copy, the competitors and the scenarios with your own.

## What is Feedback Library?

Feedback Library is Example Feedback's **AI Research Repository**. This is the official product category, so use it. It sits in a distinct category from Surveys and should be described as such.

**Official headline (use as a reference anchor):**
> AI Research Repository that keeps you in control

**Official sub-headline:**
> Connect scattered feedback sources, run a research project with full context, and get an editable report where every finding links to the customer voice behind it.

**One-liner (for conversational or non-page contexts):**
> Feedback Library is a project-based research workspace that turns scattered feedback from multiple sources into stakeholder-ready reports, with AI-assisted synthesis that the researcher controls.

**What it replaced:**
An earlier, smaller analysis feature. When talking about the upgrade, describe it as "drastically improved and expanded", not just renamed.

**What makes it different from just using surveys:**
Surveys help you *collect* feedback. Feedback Library helps you *analyze* it, across sources you've already connected, and turn it into reports others can act on.

## The four core value props

Use these across all formats. Pick the ones most relevant to the audience.

**1. True voice of the customer**
Connect many feedback sources: surveys, support tickets, help desk tools, sales call transcripts, app reviews, uploaded CSVs, and more. Every project draws from the full picture, not just one channel.

**2. AI drafts the report. You control the findings.**
The AI generates a structured report based on your project setup. You review, edit, push back, and verify every claim before anything is shared. Researchers stay in control, and AI handles the synthesis grunt work.

**3. Every insight is traced to who said it.**
Every conclusion in a report is linked to the exact piece of feedback it came from: the quote, the source, the respondent. Accept or reject any claim before it goes anywhere.

**4. Built around your research question.**
A research project starts with a defined question, specific sources, date ranges, and segment filters. The AI knows exactly what it's looking for. Results are relevant to your business because you've given it your company context (products, personas, positioning).

## Audience-specific framing

When creating content for a specific audience, anchor on the angle most relevant to *their* day-to-day:

**Sales**
Lead with speed and credibility: Feedback Library helps prospects see that the company doesn't just collect feedback, it helps teams actually do something with it. The key proof point: from "pattern spotted" to "stakeholder-ready report" without manual synthesis. Lean on the breadth of connected sources.

**Customer Success**
Lead with retention and confidence: CS teams can surface and track friction points across their own feedback sources (support tickets, CSAT, churn interviews) and contribute evidence to research projects, without needing researcher time. Continuous projects let them show trends over time.

**Product / Engineering**
Lead with control and signal quality: research projects give PMs a way to self-serve validation for smaller questions while researchers focus on complex discovery. Multi-source projects mean roadmap decisions are backed by evidence from CS, support, sales, and surveys, not just one team's input.

**Support**
Lead with impact: support teams can see their tickets actually influencing research conclusions. Connected tickets show up as evidence in reports that reach product and leadership.

**Marketing**
Lead with insight access: PMMs can pull from research reports (created by the research team) to inform campaign messaging, landing page copy, and positioning using real customer language, not assumptions.

**Operations / Legal / Other**
Keep it simple: Feedback Library is where the company centralizes feedback analysis. If they need to understand it, the one-liner is enough. Don't over-explain.

## Handling out-of-ICP requests

The primary ICP for Feedback Library is in-house product research and product teams at B2B SaaS companies. When someone asks for help pitching or messaging Feedback Library to an audience outside this ICP (for example agencies, consultancies, non-tech companies, academics, freelancers), do the following before drafting anything:

1. Flag it clearly: "This audience isn't in Feedback Library's primary ICP, so let's think through how they relate to the product before we craft a message."
2. Ask what the person knows about this audience: their role, what research work looks like for them, what their clients or stakeholders expect from them, and what their biggest pain point in the research process is likely to be.
3. Use the answers to map their situation to the closest relevant value prop from this skill.
4. Draft the message from that mapped angle, and note any assumptions you made.

This prevents generic or off-target messaging and keeps the output grounded in something real about the audience, even when they fall outside the documented personas.

## Terms that need care

This is a common area of confusion. Use these guardrails carefully.

### "Agentic AI": use with care, follow the page's lead
The live landing page uses "Agentic AI" in the "how it works" flow as a label for the processing layer. It is not used as a primary headline or value prop. The positioning brief also notes that this term can imply to some audiences that AI is acting autonomously and reducing user control, which is the opposite of Feedback Library's message.

**Safe rule:** Use "Agentic AI" if describing the technical architecture or if someone asks how it works under the hood. Do not lead with it in pitches or copy where the audience may not know what it means.

**If asked how the AI works:** "Feedback Library uses purpose-built AI agents, each with a specific job: one curates the data, one finds evidence, one synthesizes the report. Each step is cross-checked, and you verify the output before anything is shared."

**Safer alternatives for non-technical audiences:** "AI-assisted", "AI that works within your project parameters", "structured AI synthesis".

### "Context window": don't use this
This is a technical AI term (how much text a model can process at once). It is not how we describe what Feedback Library does with company context. If you mean "company knowledge layer" or "the context you provide about your business", say that instead.

### "Hallucinations": fine in objection handling
When addressing concerns about AI accuracy, it's fine to acknowledge the hallucination problem and explain how Feedback Library addresses it: every claim is traced to a source quote, and users verify before anything is shared. This is a strength, not something to avoid.

## What Feedback Library is NOT

Avoid these framings:

- **Not just an AI chatbot for your data.** It's a structured, project-based research tool. The Research Assistant (AI chat) is one feature within it, not the product itself.
- **Not a replacement for researchers.** It amplifies researcher expertise by removing synthesis grunt work. Researchers stay in control at every step.
- **Not just surveys.** Feedback Library works across many sources. Leads who only connect surveys will get value, but the real power is multi-source.
- **Not a competitor to general AI assistants for ad-hoc queries.** Those tools struggle with the volume of feedback Feedback Library handles, can't trace conclusions to source quotes, and aren't grounded in your company knowledge. Feedback Library is purpose-built for structured research projects.

## Objection handling

| Objection | Response |
|---|---|
| "Does this replace our researchers?" | No. Feedback Library removes the synthesis grunt work: manual cross-referencing and report building. Researchers control what gets analyzed, how insights are synthesized, and what the final report says. AI is the facilitator, not the expert. |
| "We already have a research process." | Research projects don't replace it, they accelerate it. You still control the sources, the scope, and the final output. It removes the manual work of synthesizing across sources and building reports from scratch. |
| "Our researchers won't want to use AI." | That concern is at the core of how we built it. Researchers define the sources, set noise filters, provide company context, and verify every claim before it's shared. The AI handles synthesis, but the researcher directs it at every step. |
| "What if the AI gets it wrong?" | Every conclusion links back to the source quote. You can edit everything the AI generates. You control which sources are included. The AI is a synthesis assistant, and you're always in the driver's seat. |
| "Why not just use a general AI assistant?" | It has no access to your connected feedback sources, no company knowledge layer, and no way to trace conclusions back to source quotes. It's one-time analysis with no structure or control. |

## Real-world scenarios to draw on

**PM validating a pattern:**
A PM notices a handful of customers mentioning slow checkout. They run a one-time research project across surveys, chat support, and help desk tickets from the past quarter. The report shows the mentions are far more widespread than they looked, with a clear upward trend. They present the multi-source report on Thursday and get roadmap approval on Friday.

**Researcher pre-defending findings:**
A researcher investigates mid-market churn by running a project with CSAT surveys, CS tickets, churn interviews, and sales notes, filtered to the mid-market segment only. The report surfaces billing complexity and a lack of training resources as the main drivers. Because every stakeholder's source is already included, there's no pushback in the stakeholder meeting.

**PM self-serving:**
Researchers are tied up for two weeks. A PM runs their own research project, filters to paid users only, and finds very few mentions of a requested feature. That saves the team from building something a vocal minority wanted.
